# Slide 1 — Fundamentals of Research Methodology

### **Fundamentals of Research Methodology**
- **Presented by:** D. K. Bhattacharyya  
- **Department:** Computer Science & Engineering, School of Engineering, Tezpur University  

### Simple meaning:
**Research methodology** is the systematic, structured path used to conduct valid research. It encompasses:
- Understanding what research genuinely is.
- Knowing how to identify and formulate a sound research problem.
- Systematically carrying out experiments and theoretical investigations.
- Writing and organizing a thesis document according to academic standards.
- Ethically presenting, publishing, and referencing research contributions.

# Slide 2 — Outline & What is Needed for a Ph.D.?

### **What is Needed to Get a Ph.D.?**
The presentation defines the core doctoral requirement:
> *"A systematic and planned approach to achieve something relevant and significant in a defined field of research in a comfortable means."*

### Simple meaning:
To earn a Ph.D., you cannot work randomly or wait for lucky accidents. You need:
1. **A defined field**: Concentrating on a specific, well-bounded area.
2. **Relevance**: Your work must address an actual open question in that field.
3. **Significance**: The outcome must make an important, non-trivial difference.
4. **A systematic approach**: Following an organized, logical method rather than haphazard guessing.
5. **Comfortable means**: Using sustainable, structured timelines and working habits without burning out.

# Slide 3 — What is Graduate Research?

### **Graduate Research**
The most defining characteristic of graduate and doctoral research is:
> **An original contribution to knowledge.**

### What does "original contribution" mean?
It means producing something new that advances human understanding. You are not simply learning or summarizing what is already known; you are adding a new brick to the wall of knowledge:
- A new algorithm, model, or theoretical framework.
- An improved, more efficient solution to an existing problem.
- Novel empirical findings, datasets, or experimental validations.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Purpose of the Thesis**: A thesis is a formal examination document. Its sole purpose is to provide clear proof that you have made an original contribution to knowledge. If your thesis fails to demonstrate an original contribution, you cannot be awarded a Ph.D.

---

### **The Two Cardinal Tests of a Thesis**
Your thesis must decisively prove two things:

1. **You identified a worthwhile problem**:
   - The question is meaningful, non-trivial, and not already adequately solved by others.
2. **You solved the problem / answered the question**:
   - Finding a gap is not enough; you must provide a rigorous, verifiable solution.

```text
Identify a worthwhile open problem 
           ↓
Conduct independent investigation 
           ↓
Formulate verifiable solution 
           ↓
Contribute new, original knowledge
```

# Slide 4 — What is the Graduate Thesis? (The Examiner's Mindset)

When external examiners read your thesis, they evaluate four fundamental questions:

### 1. What is the student's research question?
- Is the problem statement clear, precise, and unambiguous?
- Can the reader immediately identify what problem is being investigated?

### 2. Is it a good question?
- Has someone already answered this question?
- Is it worth spending 3 to 4 years of public or university resources on?
- Does solving it actually help the scientific community or industry?

### 3. Did the student prove that the question was adequately answered?
- Did you supply rigorous proof, thorough experiments, or verifiable empirical data?
- Simply asserting *"my method is better"* is unacceptable; you must provide comparative benchmarks against state-of-the-art methods.

### 4. Has the student made an adequate contribution to knowledge?
- Did the work push the boundaries of the field forward?
- Are the findings substantial enough to merit a doctoral degree?

# Slide 5 — What is a Thesis? (Criteria for Doctoral Quality)

A doctoral thesis must demonstrate three core attributes:

1. **Independent Enquiry**: You formulated hypotheses, designed experiments, and analyzed findings through your own intellectual effort rather than relying on hand-holding.
2. **Originality**: The methodology, analytical framework, or conclusions contain novel insights not previously published.
3. **New Contribution to Knowledge**: Tangible progress in your specific domain.

> [!TIP]
> **The Practical Benchmark of Doctoral Significance**:  
> A standard rule of thumb is that your thesis should contain enough valuable, original material that multiple peer-reviewed research papers can be **distilled** (extracted) and published in reputable scientific journals.

### The Role of Your Supervisor:
Your supervisor helps calibrate expectations regarding originality and depth. A great practical starting point is studying recent high-quality Ph.D. theses produced within your department or field.

# Slide 6 — Choosing a Research Project

The presentation emphasizes:
> *"Choosing a research project is the most difficult task facing a new entrant!"*

### Step 1: Choose a General Research Area
Start with a broader field of interest in Computer Science & Engineering, such as:
- Artificial Intelligence / Machine Learning
- Network Security / Cybersecurity
- Data Mining / Knowledge Discovery
- Cloud & Distributed Systems
- Image Processing & Computer Vision

### Step 2: Define a Specific Project with Your Supervisor
A new entrant lacks the perspective to know what is feasible. Your supervisor evaluates two critical dimensions:
- **Worthiness**: Is the topic significant enough to justify a Ph.D.?
- **Do-ability**: Can the project realistically be completed with available tools, data, and time?

> [!WARNING]
> **The Beginner's Paradox**:  
> New research students almost always **underestimate worthiness** (*"this small problem doesn't look grand enough"*) and **overestimate do-ability** (*"I will build an entirely new operating system in 6 months"*). Remember: *modest-sounding projects prove much harder than they look.*

---

# Slide 7 — Criteria for a Research Project

A viable Ph.D. project must satisfy five mandatory criteria:

1. **Personal Enthusiasm**: You must be genuinely fascinated by the problem; passion is the only fuel that sustains you through years of setbacks.
2. **Worthy of a Ph.D.**: It must be deep enough to yield significant contributions, not just routine engineering tasks.
3. **Close to the State of the Art**: It must be situated right at the current research frontier so that small advances push beyond existing boundaries.
4. **Doable in Three Years**: It must have a realistic scope that can be fully investigated and written up within 3 to 4 years.
5. **Supervisory Support**: There must be an experienced faculty member willing and qualified to guide your investigation.

# Slide 8 — Roadmap: Standard Pitfalls

To prepare students for the research journey, the lecture introduces nine classic methodological traps (fully detailed in Module 2):

1. **Solving the World**: Choosing an impossibly broad topic instead of a focused problem.
2. **Manna from Heaven**: Staring at a blank wall expecting inspiration to fall from the sky.
3. **Computer Bum**: Spending endless months tweaking code and graphics while ignoring theory.
4. **Yet Another Language**: Getting distracted by designing unnecessary tools or languages.
5. **Micawberism**: Running aimless experiments hoping something useful magically turns up.
6. **Ivory Tower**: Isolating yourself from related fields, literature, and peers.
7. **Misunderstood Genius**: Blaming communication failures on other people's lack of intelligence.
8. **Lost in Abstraction**: Getting trapped in theoretical formalisms that have no practical relevance.
9. **Ambition Paralysis**: Demanding perfection on draft one and becoming paralyzed to begin.

---

# Slide 9 — Roadmap: Psychological Hurdles

The lecture outlines six internal emotional challenges researchers encounter (fully detailed in Module 3):

1. **Mental Attitude**: Cultivating strong belief in your work while remaining humble and receptive to criticism.
2. **Research Impotence**: Self-doubt and the imposter syndrome believing you lack "innate research talent."
3. **Dealing with Criticism**: Distinguishing constructive critique from misunderstanding, and handling rejections gracefully.
4. **Early Morning — Cold Start**: Procrastination and overcoming the friction of beginning daily work.
5. **Theorem Envy**: Inappropriately forcing elegant mathematical proofs onto problems where empirical or engineering methods are needed.
6. **Fear of Exposure**: Hesitating to test your cherished ideas because you dread discovering they might fail.
