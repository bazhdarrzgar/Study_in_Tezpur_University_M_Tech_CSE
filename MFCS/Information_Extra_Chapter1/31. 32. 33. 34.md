Yes 👍 These four rules look complicated, but they are actually based on **two simple ideas**:

* **Transpose = flip rows and columns**
* **Inverse = undo multiplication**

Let's go one by one with small examples and complete step-by-step calculations.

---

# 31. Transpose of $AB$

The rule is:

$$
\boxed{(AB)^T=B^TA^T}
$$

### ⭐ The important part:

**The order reverses.**

Normally:

$$
AB
$$

After transpose:

$$
\boxed{B^TA^T}
$$

NOT:

$$
A^TB^T
$$

---

## Example

Let:

$$
A=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2\\
3&4
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
B=
\begin{bmatrix}
5&6\\
7&8
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Step 1: Calculate $AB$

$$
AB=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2\\
3&4
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
5&6\\
7&8
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Middle calculations (Row $\times$ Column):**
* **Row 1 × Col 1:** $(1)(5) + (2)(7) = 5 + 14 = 19$
* **Row 1 × Col 2:** $(1)(6) + (2)(8) = 6 + 16 = 22$
* **Row 2 × Col 1:** $(3)(5) + (4)(7) = 15 + 28 = 43$
* **Row 2 × Col 2:** $(3)(6) + (4)(8) = 18 + 32 = 50$

So:

$$
AB=
\begin{bmatrix}
19&22\\
43&50
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Step 2: Transpose $AB$

Swap rows into columns:

$$
(AB)^T=
\begin{bmatrix}
19&43\\
22&50
\end{bmatrix}
$$

---

### Step 3: Now calculate $B^T A^T$ (using the rule)

First transpose each matrix individually:

* Rows of $B$ become columns of $B^T$:
  $$
  B^T=
  \begin{bmatrix}
  5&7\\
  6&8
  \end{bmatrix}
  $$

* Rows of $A$ become columns of $A^T$:
  $$
  A^T=
  \begin{bmatrix}
  1&3\\
  2&4
  \end{bmatrix}
  $$

Now multiply $B^T A^T$:

$$
B^TA^T=
\begin{bmatrix}
5&7\\
6&8
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
1&3\\
2&4
\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Middle calculations (Row $\times$ Column):**
* **Row 1 × Col 1:** $(5)(1) + (7)(2) = 5 + 14 = 19$
* **Row 1 × Col 2:** $(5)(3) + (7)(4) = 15 + 28 = 43$
* **Row 2 × Col 1:** $(6)(1) + (8)(2) = 6 + 16 = 22$
* **Row 2 × Col 2:** $(6)(3) + (8)(4) = 18 + 32 = 50$

So:

$$
B^TA^T=
\begin{bmatrix}
19&43\\
22&50
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Conclusion:

$$
(AB)^T = B^TA^T =
\begin{bmatrix}
19&43\\
22&50
\end{bmatrix}
\quad \checkmark
$$

Same answer!

### 🧠 Memorize:

$$
\boxed{(AB)^T=B^TA^T}
$$

**Transpose → reverse the order.**

# 32. Inverse and Transpose

The rule:

$$
\boxed{(A^T)^{-1}=(A^{-1})^T}
$$

This says:

> You can **transpose first, then inverse**, or **inverse first, then transpose**.
> Both give the exact same answer.

---

## Numerical Example

Let's use the matrix:

$$
A =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 \\
3 & 4
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Recall the standard formula for the inverse of a $2 \times 2$ matrix:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a & b \\
c & d
\end{bmatrix}^{-1}
=
\frac{1}{ad - bc}
\begin{bmatrix}
d & -b \\
-c & a
\end{bmatrix}
$$

---

### Way 1: Transpose first, then invert ($A \rightarrow A^T \rightarrow (A^T)^{-1}$)

1. **Transpose $A$:**
   $$
   A^T =
   \begin{bmatrix}
   1 & 3 \\
   2 & 4
   \end{bmatrix}
   $$

2. **Invert $A^T$:**
   * Determinant: $(1)(4) - (3)(2) = 4 - 6 = -2$
   * Swap diagonal entries and negate off-diagonal entries:
     $$
     \begin{bmatrix}
     4 & -3 \\
     -2 & 1
     \end{bmatrix}
     $$
   * Multiply by $\frac{1}{\text{determinant}} = \frac{1}{-2} = -\frac{1}{2}$:
     $$
     (A^T)^{-1} =
     -\frac{1}{2}
     \begin{bmatrix}
     4 & -3 \\
     -2 & 1
     \end{bmatrix}
     =
     \begin{bmatrix}
     \frac{4}{-2} & \frac{-3}{-2} \\[6pt]
     \frac{-2}{-2} & \frac{1}{-2}
     \end{bmatrix}
     =
     \begin{bmatrix}
     -2 & \frac{3}{2} \\[6pt]
     1 & -\frac{1}{2}
     \end{bmatrix}
     $$

---

### Way 2: Invert first, then transpose ($A \rightarrow A^{-1} \rightarrow (A^{-1})^T$)

1. **Invert $A$:**
   * Determinant: $(1)(4) - (2)(3) = 4 - 6 = -2$
   * Swap diagonal entries and negate off-diagonal entries:
     $$
     \begin{bmatrix}
     4 & -2 \\
     -3 & 1
     \end{bmatrix}
     $$
   * Multiply by $-\frac{1}{2}$:
     $$
     A^{-1} =
     -\frac{1}{2}
     \begin{bmatrix}
     4 & -2 \\
     -3 & 1
     \end{bmatrix}
     =
     \begin{bmatrix}
     -2 & 1 \\[6pt]
     \frac{3}{2} & -\frac{1}{2}
     \end{bmatrix}
     $$

2. **Transpose $A^{-1}$ (rows become columns):**
   * Row 1 $\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \longrightarrow$ Column 1: $\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
   * Row 2 $\begin{bmatrix} \frac{3}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix} \longrightarrow$ Column 2: $\begin{bmatrix} \frac{3}{2} \\ -\frac{1}{2} \end{bmatrix}$

   $$
   (A^{-1})^T =
   \begin{bmatrix}
   -2 & \frac{3}{2} \\[6pt]
   1 & -\frac{1}{2}
   \end{bmatrix}
   $$

---

### Comparison:

$$
\boxed{(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T =
\begin{bmatrix}
-2 & \frac{3}{2} \\[6pt]
1 & -\frac{1}{2}
\end{bmatrix}}
\quad \checkmark
$$

Both ways produce the exact same matrix!

# 33. Symmetric Matrix

A matrix is symmetric when:

$$
\boxed{A^T=A}
$$

Meaning:

> If you transpose it, **nothing changes**.

### Example $2 \times 2$:

$$
A=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2\\
2&8
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Transpose (rows become columns):

$$
A^T=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2\\
2&8
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Notice $A^T = A$, so $A$ is **symmetric**. ✅

---

### Easy visual check:

Look at the main diagonal:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\boxed{1}&2\\
2&\boxed{8}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The numbers across the diagonal mirror each other:

$$
\boxed{a_{12} = a_{21} = 2}
$$

---

### Example $3 \times 3$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\color{red}{1}&2&3\\
2&\color{red}{5}&6\\
3&6&\color{red}{9}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Check the mirrored entries across the red diagonal:
* $a_{12} = a_{21} = 2$
* $a_{13} = a_{31} = 3$
* $a_{23} = a_{32} = 6$

Every pair matches, so it is symmetric.

# 34. $R^TR$ and $RR^T$

For **any matrix $R$** (even if $R$ is rectangular and not symmetric):

$$
\boxed{R^TR}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\boxed{RR^T}
$$

are **always**:
1. **Square**
2. **Symmetric**

---

## Concrete Example with Full Calculations

Let:

$$
R=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2\\
3&4\\
5&6
\end{bmatrix}
\quad (3 \times 2)
$$

Its transpose is:

$$
R^T=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&3&5\\
2&4&6
\end{bmatrix}
\quad (2 \times 3)
$$

---

### Part 1: Calculate $R^TR$

$$
R^TR=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&3&5\\
2&4&6
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2\\
3&4\\
5&6
\end{bmatrix}
$$

* **Size check:** $(2\times 3) \times (3\times 2) \longrightarrow 2\times 2$ (it is **square**!)

**Middle calculations (Row $\times$ Column):**
* **Row 1 × Col 1:** $(1)(1) + (3)(3) + (5)(5) = 1 + 9 + 25 = 35$
* **Row 1 × Col 2:** $(1)(2) + (3)(4) + (5)(6) = 2 + 12 + 30 = 44$
* **Row 2 × Col 1:** $(2)(1) + (4)(3) + (6)(5) = 2 + 12 + 30 = 44$
* **Row 2 × Col 2:** $(2)(2) + (4)(4) + (6)(6) = 4 + 16 + 36 = 56$

So:

$$
\boxed{R^TR=
\begin{bmatrix}
35 & 44 \\
44 & 56
\end{bmatrix}}
$$

Look at the off-diagonal numbers:
$$
a_{12} = 44, \quad a_{21} = 44 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad a_{12} = a_{21}
$$
It is **symmetric**! ✅

---

### Part 2: Calculate $RR^T$

$$
RR^T=
\begin{bmatrix}
1&2\\
3&4\\
5&6
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
1&3&5\\
2&4&6
\end{bmatrix}
$$

* **Size check:** $(3\times 2) \times (2\times 3) \longrightarrow 3\times 3$ (it is **square**!)

**Middle calculations (Row $\times$ Column):**
* **Row 1 × Col 1:** $(1)(1) + (2)(2) = 1 + 4 = 5$
* **Row 1 × Col 2:** $(1)(3) + (2)(4) = 3 + 8 = 11$
* **Row 1 × Col 3:** $(1)(5) + (2)(6) = 5 + 12 = 17$
* **Row 2 × Col 1:** $(3)(1) + (4)(2) = 3 + 8 = 11$
* **Row 2 × Col 2:** $(3)(3) + (4)(4) = 9 + 16 = 25$
* **Row 2 × Col 3:** $(3)(5) + (4)(6) = 15 + 24 = 39$
* **Row 3 × Col 1:** $(5)(1) + (6)(2) = 5 + 12 = 17$
* **Row 3 × Col 2:** $(5)(3) + (6)(4) = 15 + 24 = 39$
* **Row 3 × Col 3:** $(5)(5) + (6)(6) = 25 + 36 = 61$

So:

$$
\boxed{RR^T=
\begin{bmatrix}
5 & 11 & 17 \\
11 & 25 & 39 \\
17 & 39 & 61
\end{bmatrix}}
$$

Look at the mirrored pairs:
* $a_{12} = a_{21} = 11$
* $a_{13} = a_{31} = 17$
* $a_{23} = a_{32} = 39$

Every single pair matches, so $RR^T$ is **symmetric**! ✅

---

### Why is it ALWAYS symmetric algebraically?

Let's test by taking the transpose of $R^TR$:

$$
(R^TR)^T
$$

Using the transpose product rule $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$:

$$
(R^TR)^T = R^T (R^T)^T
$$

Since transposing twice gives back the original matrix: $(R^T)^T = R$:

$$
(R^TR)^T = R^TR
$$

Because $(R^TR)^T = R^TR$, the matrix is **always symmetric**.

The exact same logic applies to $RR^T$:

$$
(RR^T)^T = (R^T)^T R^T = RR^T
$$

---

# 🧠 SUPER SIMPLE CHEAT SHEET

### ① Transpose product

$$
\boxed{(AB)^T=B^TA^T}
$$

**Reverse the order!**

---

### ② Inverse + transpose

$$
\boxed{(A^T)^{-1}=(A^{-1})^T}
$$

**Either order gives the same answer.**

---

### ③ Symmetric

$$
\boxed{A^T=A}
$$

**Transpose doesn't change the matrix.**

---

### ④ $R^TR$ and $RR^T$

$$
\boxed{R^TR \text{ and } RR^T \text{ are always square and symmetric}}
$$

3. $\boxed{A^T=A \quad\Rightarrow\quad\text{symmetric}}$
4. $\boxed{R^TR \text{ and } RR^T \text{ are square and symmetric}}$
